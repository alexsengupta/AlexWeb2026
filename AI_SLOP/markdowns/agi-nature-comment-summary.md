# Have We Already Achieved AGI? A Bold Claim in *Nature*

## Summary

In a Comment piece published in *Nature* on 5 February 2026, philosophers and computer scientists Eddy Keming Chen, Mikhail Belkin, Leon Bergen, and David Danks argue that artificial general intelligence (AGI) has already arrived — and that the scientific community needs to stop moving the goalposts and acknowledge it.

The authors contend that by any reasonable standard anchored to Turing's original 1950 vision, current large language models meet the threshold for general intelligence. They point to GPT-4.5 passing a Turing test more convincingly than actual humans, LLMs earning gold medals at the International Mathematical Olympiad, solving PhD-level exam problems across disciplines, generating experimentally validated scientific hypotheses, and assisting with frontier research.

Their core definitional move is to argue that general intelligence requires sufficient *breadth* (competence across multiple domains) and *depth* (strong performance within them), calibrated against what we'd accept from individual humans. They explicitly argue that perfection, universality, embodiment, agency, self-awareness, and economic productivity are not prerequisites — and that demanding these features either excludes most humans or reflects anthropocentric bias.

The authors then systematically address ten common objections: that LLMs are "stochastic parrots," lack world models, only understand words, lack bodies, lack agency, lack identity, are inefficient learners, hallucinate, lack economic value, or possess an "alien" intelligence that doesn't count. In each case, they argue the objection either sets a bar humans themselves wouldn't clear, or has been empirically overtaken by recent model capabilities.

## Claude's Critique

The piece is well-structured and makes some genuinely useful points — particularly the observation that sceptics who perpetually predict failure "just beyond current achievements" are not offering a falsifiable scientific position. The comparison to how we assess intelligence in other humans (behaviorally, not by inspecting internal mechanisms) is also apt.

That said, the argument has notable gaps. The authors largely sidestep the question of *understanding* versus *performance*, treating behavioral output as sufficient evidence for intelligence while acknowledging in passing that we can't resolve the deeper philosophical question. Their dismissal of the "stochastic parrot" critique leans heavily on pointing to impressive outputs rather than engaging with the mechanistic question of whether LLMs are doing something qualitatively different from sophisticated pattern completion. The suggestion that human intelligence might itself be "a sophisticated version of a stochastic parrot" is a rhetorical move, not an argument — it dissolves the question rather than answering it.

Perhaps most critically, the 76% of leading AI researchers surveyed who doubt current approaches will yield AGI are acknowledged but essentially dismissed as conceptually confused or emotionally biased. That's a strong claim to make against such a large expert majority without more substantive engagement with *why* those researchers hold that view.

The piece is worth reading as a clear, accessible articulation of the "AGI is here" position. Whether you find it convincing may depend on whether you think passing difficult tests is the same thing as possessing the capacity those tests were designed to measure.
