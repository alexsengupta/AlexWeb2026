# Don't Let AI Climb the Hill for Our Kids

## Summary

In a blog post published in *Teachers on Fire Magazine* on Medium in January 2026, high school teacher David Cutler uses a morning sledding outing with his seven-year-old son to argue that generative AI threatens student development by short-circuiting productive struggle. After nearly two feet of snow fell in the Boston area, Cutler watched his son attempt to climb an icy hill — slipping, adjusting his footing, and eventually reaching the top unaided. Cutler treats this as a microcosm of how learning should work: through effort, failure, and self-correction rather than external intervention.

The theoretical scaffolding draws on two sources. First, Nassim Nicholas Taleb's concept of "antifragility" from *Antifragile: Things That Gain from Disorder* — the idea that certain systems grow stronger through stress rather than in spite of it. Second, Jonathan Haidt's work in *The Coddling of the American Mind* on the developmental costs of shielding young people from difficulty. Cutler quotes a 2019 interview with Haidt distinguishing propositional learning (information delivered by adults) from experiential learning (feedback from doing things, including getting hurt), with Haidt arguing the latter is faster and deeper.

Cutler maps this framework onto AI use in classrooms: when students receive structure, phrasing, or answers from generative AI before wrestling with a problem themselves, they complete tasks without gaining the experiential feedback that builds judgment, resilience, and independence. He likens this to lifting a child up a hill — the destination is reached, but the developmental value of the climb is lost.

Cutler is careful to note that the danger is not AI's existence but its *timing*. Used too early in a learning process, AI replaces effort rather than augmenting it. He frames this not as technophobia but as a pedagogical concern about sequencing: students should demonstrate independent capability before AI enters the workflow.

## Claude's Critique

The hill metaphor is intuitive and emotionally resonant, and the timing distinction is more nuanced than the blanket anti-AI stance common in this genre. But the argument has significant limitations.

The entire case rests on analogy: climbing an icy hill is treated as structurally equivalent to writing an essay, solving a math problem, or reasoning through ambiguity. These are not the same kind of task. Physical challenges offer immediate, unambiguous feedback (you slip or you don't). Cognitive and creative tasks involve far more complex feedback loops, and the role of external scaffolding — including tools — in developing higher-order thinking is well-established in educational psychology. Vygotsky's zone of proximal development is the obvious framework Cutler never engages with. The question isn't whether struggle matters — it obviously does — but *which kinds of struggle are productive* and at what point tool use transitions from crutch to lever. Cutler doesn't address this, treating all AI assistance before independent mastery as equally corrosive.

The reliance on Taleb and Haidt also papers over complexity. Taleb's antifragility framework was developed for financial and systemic risk, not pedagogy, and its application to child development is metaphorical rather than empirical. Haidt's work on overprotection has faced substantive criticism for overstating causal claims from correlational data. Neither source provides evidence specifically about AI's effect on learning outcomes; Cutler is extrapolating from general principles about struggle to a specific technology whose educational effects are still being actively studied.

The piece also conspicuously lacks engagement with the growing body of research on AI-assisted learning, including studies suggesting that well-scaffolded AI use can increase the quality of student revision, deepen metacognitive awareness, and free cognitive resources for higher-order tasks. Worth reading as an honest articulation of a common teacher concern; less convincing as a basis for policy.
