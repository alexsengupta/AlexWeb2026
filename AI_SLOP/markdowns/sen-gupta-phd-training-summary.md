# PhD Training Needs a Reboot in an AI World

## Summary

In an essay published in *Nature* on 6 November 2025, oceanographer Alex Sen Gupta argues that AI is not merely another tool for researchers but a transformative force that is redefining what doctoral training means — and that universities are mostly unprepared for the scale of disruption ahead.

Sen Gupta's central claim is that AI models are rapidly acquiring the ability to perform much of the conventional PhD workflow: drafting literature reviews, writing code, generating hypotheses, analysing data, and even drafting manuscripts. To illustrate this, the essay describes a role-play exercise in which Sen Gupta, playing a PhD student, interacted with Claude (Anthropic's chatbot) configured as a fictional near-future AI research assistant called "HALe." In a single unedited session, HALe searched databases, extracted and harmonised data, ran machine learning analyses, interpreted results, drafted a manuscript, suggested reviewers, and created a data repository — compressing months of doctoral labour into a few hours of guided exchanges.

The essay acknowledges that current AI systems cannot yet perform these tasks with full autonomy, and that experimental or field-based PhDs remain less susceptible to automation. But the trajectory is clear enough to demand action. Sen Gupta argues that training must shift from executing research tasks to framing questions, critically evaluating AI outputs, and maintaining oversight of automated workflows. He flags a core dilemma: students need precisely the skills AI is replacing in order to identify when AI produces plausible but incorrect outputs.

Practical implications follow. Assessment may need to pivot from polished written theses toward oral defences and live problem-solving. Programme structures may need rethinking — should PhDs be shortened, or expanded in scope? And institutions without resources to provide AI access risk deepening inequities in research capacity. Sen Gupta notes that some universities (Oxford, Nanyang Technological University, Karolinska Institute) have begun issuing research-specific AI policies and competency courses, but argues these mostly treat AI as a powerful tool rather than addressing what happens when it takes over core research functions.

## Claude's Critique

The essay is well-pitched and practically grounded, and Sen Gupta deserves credit for actually running the simulation rather than speculating abstractly. The accompanying supplementary material — a full transcript of the HALe role-play — is genuinely illuminating as a concrete demonstration of what near-future AI-assisted research might look like.

That said, the piece has some notable analytical gaps. The HALe simulation, while vivid, is somewhat self-serving as evidence: a chatbot prompted to behave as a hyper-competent research assistant will, unsurprisingly, behave as a hyper-competent research assistant. The exercise demonstrates what a plausible *script* of AI-assisted research looks like, not what actually works in practice. The fictional data, fictional analyses, and fictional results cannot tell us anything about whether AI systems reliably produce correct outputs when operating on real, messy scientific problems — which is precisely the question that matters for doctoral training.

The essay also underplays a harder problem lurking beneath the surface: if students lack the domain expertise to evaluate AI outputs (because they never developed it through hands-on research), then "critical evaluation of AI-generated work" becomes an empty competency. Sen Gupta acknowledges this as a "dilemma" but doesn't grapple seriously with whether it's actually solvable within the framework he proposes. There's a risk of a vicious circle in which AI accelerates research while eroding the human capacity to verify it.

Finally, the piece is notably light on engagement with the substantial literature on expertise acquisition and deliberate practice. The cognitive science of skill development suggests that compressing or bypassing effortful practice has real consequences for deep understanding — a point that deserves more than a passing mention of "intellectual atrophy." The question isn't just whether PhDs *can* be AI-assisted, but whether the resulting graduates will have the intellectual foundations to lead research independently.
