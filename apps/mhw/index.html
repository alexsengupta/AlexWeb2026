About this explainer

The question

As the ocean warms, marine heatwaves (MHWs) become more common. If warming is steady, you might expect the number of heatwave days to grow steadily too. It doesn't: even with perfectly linear warming, heatwave days accelerate. This explainer shows why, using three made-up temperature series and then a real one.

How it works

Each example starts with a temperature series and a fixed threshold. A day is a heatwave day when the temperature is above it. We then add the same warming, μ, to every day, a little at a time, and recount. The threshold is never updated, as is standard when measuring change against a fixed historical baseline.

For the made-up series, every run of days above the threshold counts as one event, and intensity is measured above the series' normal level (zero).

For the real series we use the standard definition of Hobday et al. (2016):

  • Climatology and threshold: the normal temperature (climatology) and the 90th-percentile threshold for each day of the year are calculated from 1982–2011, using an 11-day window and then smoothed over 31 days.
  • Events: an event needs at least 5 days in a row above the threshold, and events separated by gaps of 2 days or less are joined.
  • Duration and MHW days: duration runs from an event's first day to its last, and MHW days are the total of all event durations.
  • Peak intensity: the largest temperature above climatology within each event, averaged over all events.

The real data

Daily NOAA OISST v2.1 sea surface temperature at a single grid point in the central North Pacific (about 30°N, 180°E), from 1982 to September 2025, with 29 February removed. The series' own warming trend (about 0.34 °C per decade) is removed first, so the only warming is the uniform μ added here. The time-series panel shows 2022–2024, but every number uses the full record. MHW days are given per year and events per decade.

What the real example shows

  • Heatwave days go from about 24 a year with no added warming, to 150 a year with 1 °C, 300 with 2 °C and 355 with 3 °C. The rise speeds up, then levels off because a year has only 365 days.
  • Events rise from about 16 to a peak of about 62 per decade at roughly 1.1 °C, then fall as separate events merge into longer ones.
  • Average duration climbs slowly at first (14 to 25 days by 1 °C), then sharply, as merging takes over.
  • Average peak intensity rises much more slowly than the warming: 1.9 °C with no added warming and only 2.1 °C at 1 °C. Every individual heatwave peak gets μ warmer, but the many new, weaker events that start to qualify pull the average down.
