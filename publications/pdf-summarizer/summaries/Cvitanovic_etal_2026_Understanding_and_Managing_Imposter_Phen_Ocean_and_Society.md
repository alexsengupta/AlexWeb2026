## Quick Summary (100 words)

Feeling like a fraud can affect marine scientists at any career stage—even experienced professors. Drawing on 314 combined years of experience, the authors explore what triggers these feelings and how to manage them. They describe pressures including competitive workplaces, discrimination, caregiving demands, and narrow ideas about who belongs—for example, feeling inadequate because you cannot swim or dive. These doubts can lead researchers to avoid opportunities, withdraw from colleagues, or consider leaving science. Supportive mentors, therapy, boundaries, and inclusive workplaces helped the authors cope. ## Detailed Briefing (800-1000 words)

## 1. Background/Context

Feeling like a fraud despite clear evidence of achievement can be more than a passing confidence wobble. Known as **imposter phenomenon**, it involves persistent self-doubt, attributing success to luck, and fearing that others will discover you are not as capable as they think. First described by psychologists Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes in **1978**, the concept now applies across genders, professions, and career stages.

This commentary, published in *Ocean and Society* on **15 July 2026**, explores how these feelings affect marine scientists. Their work often involves much more than research: they may explain climate change publicly, advise governments, negotiate fisheries disputes, or undertake demanding fieldwork. Scientific uncertainty can become politically contentious, while hierarchical or exclusionary workplaces can make researchers question whether they belong.

The authors address a gap: although imposter feelings are widely discussed in academia, marine scientists’ lived experiences—particularly those of senior researchers—have received less open attention. They ask what triggers these feelings, how they affect careers, and what helps people manage them.

## 2. Main Research/Argument

The article brings together reflections from **14 marine researchers with 314 cumulative years of professional experience**. Their expertise spans natural and social sciences, including coral biology, oceanography, fisheries, environmental governance, and coastal livelihoods. Their institutional affiliations extend across countries including Australia, Brazil, Germany, Romania, Slovenia, and Sweden.

Importantly, this is **a reflective commentary, not a population survey or an experiment testing treatments**. Think of it as experienced colleagues comparing their professional journeys: identifying recurring obstacles while recognising that no two routes are identical. Their accounts offer concrete insights, but cannot establish how common particular experiences are across marine science.

The central argument is that imposter phenomenon is not simply an individual confidence problem. It develops through interactions between personal expectations, relationships, workplace cultures, and structural inequalities. Addressing it therefore requires both personal support and institutional change.

The authors also acknowledge **survivorship bias**: everyone contributing remained in science. Researchers whose experiences drove them out are missing, potentially leaving some of the most damaging consequences underrepresented.

## 3. Key Findings

### There is no single career trajectory

Experience does not automatically eliminate self-doubt. The authors describe **four patterns**: feelings that decrease over time, remain relatively constant, recur around particular events, or develop and intensify later in a career.

A senior appointment can therefore introduce rather than resolve insecurity. Managing projects, teaching, administration, and leadership may leave researchers feeling unable to stay sufficiently informed or contribute effectively.

The article also illustrates a self-reinforcing cycle: **high expectations lead to overwork, which produces short-term success; that success is credited to luck, increasing fear and anxiety and prompting still higher expectations**. It resembles running faster on a wheel without ever reaching a sense of security.

### Triggers reach beyond perfectionism

The authors identify **nine broad trigger categories**, including toxic workplaces, performance comparisons, disciplinary boundaries, gender bias, caregiving, cultural and class differences, internal expectations, leadership pressures, and high-stakes evaluation.

Their examples make these pressures tangible:

- Researchers crossing between social and natural sciences sometimes encountered claims that their methods were **“not real science.”**
- Women described being spoken over, ignored, or repeatedly required to demonstrate their competence.
- Non-native English speakers spent excessive time rewriting messages and presentations, worried that an accent or imperfect phrasing would be mistaken for limited expertise.
- Parenthood, pregnancy loss, health leave, and caring for elderly relatives could create a sense of falling permanently behind.
- Some questioned their identity as marine scientists because they could not **swim, snorkel, or dive**, or had not grown up boating.

That final example shows how an assumed professional “type” can exclude people whose expertise does not depend on those activities.

### Consequences include lost opportunities and lost ideas

The authors describe **seven overlapping areas of impact**. These include isolation, reduced confidence, career uncertainty, avoidance of particular professional settings, emotional and physical strain, self-sabotage, and constrained creativity.

Some avoided conferences, media interviews, collaborations, promotions, or prestigious grants. Others declined award nominations or could not bring themselves to open emails containing manuscript or funding decisions. Several considered leaving science.

The damage also affected intellectual contributions: researchers held back ambitious papers or avoided challenging established ideas despite possessing relevant expertise.

### Support works through several routes

The commentary outlines **eight broad approaches** that helped contributors. Trusted mentors, peers, and communities were central, alongside professional therapy, psychological support, leadership coaching, and mental-health literacy.

Other approaches included reframing harsh self-judgements, setting boundaries with undermining colleagues, collaborating on meaningful work, celebrating progress, and protecting sleep and physical well-being. Swimming, surfing, walking, gym sessions, and time in nature provided grounding routines for some.

These are reported experiences, not proven universal remedies, and the authors stress that the article does not replace professional mental-health care.

## 4. Significance & Real-World Implications

When researchers withdraw or leave, marine science loses knowledge, relationships, and potentially valuable solutions. This matters in fields addressing climate impacts, biodiversity loss, fisheries management, and coastal change—problems requiring multiple perspectives rather than one narrow model of expertise.

The practical response cannot simply be “be more confident.” Institutions can broaden recognition beyond publication counts, improve flexibility around caregiving, provide accessible mentoring, acknowledge language barriers, and address bullying and unequal power.

Small changes also matter. The authors describe colleagues finding ways to include someone who could not swim, challenging an unnecessary barrier to belonging. Similarly, recognising community engagement and applied research as valuable scholarship can help researchers whose contributions fall outside traditional academic expectations.

## 5. Broader Takeaways

The article’s central paradox is that doubt can sometimes support good science. Questioning assumptions, acknowledging uncertainty, and remaining open to learning can foster humility and ethical collaboration. That does **not** make chronic anxiety or exclusion beneficial; it means intellectual openness should be preserved without accepting avoidable harm.

Warning signs include persistent overwork, withdrawal, avoiding opportunities, and treating every setback as proof of inadequacy. These patterns warrant care, not judgement.

Ultimately, the goal is not to make every scientist permanently confident. It is to build research communities where uncertainty is safe to express, diverse expertise is respected, and people do not have to continually prove their right to belong.
