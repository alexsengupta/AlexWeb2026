## Quick Summary (100 words)

Can ocean heat waves make rapidly strengthening tropical cyclones more destructive? Analyzing global records from 1981–2023, researchers found that storms combining marine heat waves with rapid intensification had stronger winds, heavier rainfall, and greater economic losses. These storms accounted for 71 billion-dollar disasters, compared with 45 among rapidly intensifying storms without heat-wave influence. Higher losses persisted even when researchers compared areas with similar coastal development. Hurricane Otis, which rapidly became a Category 5 storm before striking Mexico in 2023, illustrates the danger. The findings highlight why monitoring extreme ocean warmth could improve storm warnings and help coastal communities prepare.

## Detailed Briefing (800-1000 words)

## Background/Context

Tropical cyclones become especially dangerous when they strengthen faster than people can prepare. Scientists call this **rapid intensification**: an increase in sustained wind speed of at least 30 knots—about 56 kilometres per hour—within 24 hours. Such changes can leave communities with little time to evacuate or protect infrastructure.

Hurricane Otis offered a devastating example in October 2023. Within one day, it strengthened from a tropical storm into a Category 5 hurricane before striking near Acapulco, Mexico. Its landfall winds reached 143 knots, causing approximately $16 billion in damage and at least 52 deaths. Despite improvements in forecasting, its explosive intensification was poorly predicted.

Otis, Hurricane Ian in 2022, and Super Typhoon Goni in 2020 shared another feature: they crossed ocean waters exceeding 30°C before landfall. This raises the article’s central question: **Do marine heat waves help rapidly intensifying cyclones become more destructive—and more economically damaging?**

Marine heat waves are prolonged episodes of unusually warm ocean temperatures. The researchers investigate whether these ocean extremes act as an extra energy supply for already-dangerous storms.

## Main Research/Argument

The team examined global cyclone records from 1981 to 2023, identifying 1,600 landfalling storms among 3,464 tropical cyclones. Economic damage information was available for 789 landfalling storms.

They combined storm tracks and wind measurements with daily ocean-temperature observations, atmospheric reanalysis data, and information about coastal development. Their marine heat-wave definition required at least five consecutive days above the locally relevant, seasonally changing 90th-percentile temperature threshold.

Importantly, they removed the long-term ocean-warming trend when identifying heat waves. Think of this as distinguishing an unusually hot spell from the gradual warming of the background climate: the study focused on exceptional departures from that changing background.

Storms were divided into four groups: those with both rapid intensification and marine heat-wave influence, those with rapid intensification alone, those with heat-wave influence alone, and those with neither. The researchers compared winds and rainfall from five days before landfall to two days afterward.

They also assessed **potential intensity**, the theoretical maximum strength a storm could reach under its surrounding conditions. This is like estimating an engine’s performance ceiling, rather than measuring how fast it actually runs.

To examine economic losses fairly, the researchers matched storms affecting areas with similar volumes of buildings and infrastructure. Comparing damage without this adjustment would resemble comparing identical accidents involving a bicycle and a luxury car: the value exposed matters.

Finally, they used machine learning to identify which storm and heat-wave characteristics were most useful for predicting rapid intensification over the following 24 hours.

## Key Findings

**Marine heat-wave influence was widespread.** Approximately 51.7% of landfalling cyclones—827 of 1,600 storms—were classified as heat-wave influenced. These storms encountered environments with potential intensities averaging roughly 3 to 5.5 metres per second higher during the five days before landfall.

Two cyclones striking Mozambique in 2019 illustrate the contrast. Idai, which crossed waters without marine heat waves, experienced two rapid-intensification episodes and fluctuating potential intensity, dropping to approximately 50–55 metres per second midway through its life. Kenneth crossed heat-wave regions, experienced three rapid-intensification episodes, and maintained potential intensity near 80 metres per second. These examples illustrate the broader pattern, although they do not isolate heat waves from every other influence.

**The clearest physical enhancement occurred in rapidly intensifying storms.** Those influenced by marine heat waves generally maintained winds 5–10 knots stronger than rapidly intensifying storms without that influence during much of the prelandfall period. The largest differences, around three to five days before landfall, reached approximately 15–20%.

Rainfall also increased. Rapidly intensifying, heat-wave-influenced storms showed precipitation rates approximately 10–12% higher than their non-heat-wave counterparts around landfall. Their average rainfall peaked near 3 millimetres per hour shortly before landfall. These figures describe rainfall averaged across a broad area surrounding each storm, not the heaviest local downpours.

**Economic losses showed a substantial contrast.** Among rapidly intensifying storms, 71 heat-wave-influenced cyclones caused at least $1 billion in damage, compared with 45 without heat-wave influence—about 58% more recorded billion-dollar events. Fifteen versus seven caused losses exceeding $10 billion. These are event-count comparisons, not, by themselves, evidence that an individual storm’s probability of becoming a billion-dollar disaster increased by 60%.

After matching storms for similar development exposure, rapidly intensifying cyclones with heat-wave influence caused approximately **93% higher average damage** than rapidly intensifying cyclones without it: $4.31 billion versus $2.24 billion, in 2023 dollars. For the billion-dollar subset, the corresponding difference was approximately 81%.

The machine-learning analysis ranked initial wind speed and latitude as the strongest predictors, followed by relative mean heat-wave intensity. Ocean warmth mattered, but storm conditions and location mattered too.

## Significance & Real-World Implications

The findings connect ocean-temperature extremes with tangible threats on land: stronger winds, heavier rainfall, and larger financial losses. Coastal development increases what lies in harm’s way, but the matching analysis suggests that exposure alone does not explain the damage differences.

Operational forecasting centres could incorporate marine heat-wave indicators alongside established measures such as atmospheric humidity and wind shear—the change in wind with height. Local temperature-anomaly alerts might help flag situations needing closer monitoring, although the study does not establish a ready-made warning system.

For communities, the practical message is to prepare for sudden strengthening rather than relying solely on a storm’s current category. Emergency planning, resilient infrastructure, and communication about rainfall and flooding remain essential.

## Broader Takeaways

A striking lesson is that the largest temperature anomalies are not necessarily the most dangerous for tropical cyclones. Moderate anomalies over already-hot tropical waters may be more consequential than larger anomalies at higher latitudes.

There are important limits. Disaster-loss records are incomplete, rainfall estimates can miss extremes, and matching building volume does not control every difference in wealth, vulnerability, or preparedness. The findings therefore support strong associations rather than definitive causal estimates.

Marine heat waves also cannot guarantee rapid intensification: unfavourable atmospheric conditions can still prevent it. Nevertheless, the overall warning is clear. Exceptional ocean warmth can amplify the destructive characteristics of rapidly intensifying cyclones, making ocean monitoring and faster, more flexible coastal preparedness increasingly important.
